Desk Arm V1 Design Review

A 315 mm tabletop arm built from four hobby servos, twenty printed parts and about sixty dollars. Every number below is worked from a mass budget, not guessed.

Recommendation

Build Configuration B. Direct-drive revolute arm, L1 = L2 = 120 mm, MG996R at shoulder and elbow, MG90S at base and gripper, SG90 as a software-slaved wrist leveller, ESP32 over USB serial. Worst-case shoulder safety factor is 2.44. Configuration A is genuinely viable but only by shrinking the arm to 275 mm and the payload to 30 g — and it saves roughly $8. That trade is not worth taking. Configuration D is not required; the torque margins do not justify it.

01

Recommended architecture

Serial revolute arm, the classic RRR shoulder–elbow chain on a rotating turret. Base yaw is the only vertical axis; shoulder and elbow work in a single vertical plane that the base sweeps around. That is the simplest structure that reaches every point on a desk, and the kinematics collapse to one trig problem you can write on an index card.

2 — ShoulderRevolute, horizontal0° … 110°MG996RHighest-loaded joint in the machine. Sets the whole design.
3 — ElbowRevolute, horizontal−130° … 0°MG996RElbow-up solution only; elbow-down collides with the table.

GripperTwo-finger, meshed sectors0 … 55 mmMG90SSymmetric self-centring jaws with V-grooves.

Why a slaved wrist rather than a parallelogram linkage

A three-DOF positioner has exactly enough freedom to hit an (x, y, z) target and none left over — so gripper pitch is whatever the IK happens to produce, swinging roughly 30° across the workspace. Picking a pen off a desk with tilted jaws is miserable. There are three ways out:

Twin parallelogram

Two four-bar linkages in series hold the jaws level with no extra servo, and move the elbow actuator down to the turret. Elegant, and it is what the EEZYbotARM does.

Cost: six more printed parts, twelve more pivot pins, and slop at every one of them. deferred

SG90 slaved wrist chosen

One extra micro servo at the wrist, driven by firmware as a dependent axis. You still command three DOF; the fourth is computed, never exposed.

Cost: about $3 and 17 g at 250 mm — which drops shoulder SF from 2.57 to 2.44. Cheap for level jaws.

Fixed bracket

Bolt the gripper on at a fixed 45°. Zero cost, zero parts, and it works if you always approach objects from the side.

Cost: pose-dependent jaw tilt. The right answer for Configuration A. fallback

All three bolt to the same 24 × 24 mm wrist mounting face on the forearm. You can start with the fixed bracket, add the SG90 later, and try the linkage after that without touching another part in the assembly.

04

Joint torque and servo selection

Static gravity holding torque at the worst-case pose, which for a desk arm is also the working pose — fully extended, reaching for something at the far edge. Dynamic torque is small here: at the speeds this arm will move, inertial terms are under 10% of the gravity term, and the safety factor absorbs them.

// shoulder, arm horizontal at full extension τ₁ = g · Σ mᵢdᵢ = 9.81 × 0.04507 kg·m = 0.442 N·m = 4.51 kg·cm // elbow, moments taken about the elbow axis τ₂ = 9.81 × (0.025×0.060 + 0.017×0.130 + 0.032×0.165 + 0.055×0.200) = 9.81 × 0.01999 = 0.196 N·m = 2.00 kg·cm // base yaw — no gravity term; inertia + bearing drag only I ≈ 0.0056 kg·m², α = 3.5 rad/s² → τ = 0.020 N·m, +drag ≈ 0.30 kg·cm

All figures in kg·cm. Rated values are the common published specs for generic MG996R / MG90S / SG90 at 6.0 V. On a 5 V supply the MG996R drops to ~9.4 kg·cm and shoulder SF falls to 2.08 — still acceptable, which is why a 5 V phone charger is a legitimate power fallback.

Why the elbow is over-specified, deliberately

An SF of 5.5 looks wasteful. It is not a choice — it is what the price ladder does. The elbow needs 2.00 kg·cm, so at SF 2 it needs 4.0. The MG90S tops out at 2.2 and fails. The next rung up is the MG92B at 3.5 kg·cm for about $8, which gives SF 1.75 — still short, and more expensive than an MG996R. There is nothing between them worth buying. The MG996R is the cheapest part that passes, so it wins by default and you get the margin for free.

A note on why the numbers are not tighter. Everything here is static holding torque with a stall-rated servo. Hobby servos publish stall torque, and running a servo anywhere near stall makes it hot, slow and short-lived. SF 2 is not conservatism — it is roughly the point at which an MG996R holds a pose indefinitely without cooking. Treat 2.0 as the floor, not a comfort margin.

05

Gripper: SG90 vs MG90S

Required clamping force for a 55 g object against gravity, with a conservative friction coefficient of 0.5 on a rubber pad, is mg / 2μ = 0.54 N. The design targets 4 N per jaw — about seven times what the physics demands, because real grips are off-centre and objects get bumped.

jaw tip force F = 4.0 N at 30 mm from the jaw pivot jaw pivot torque 0.12 N·m drive reduction 1.5 : 1 (12 mm servo crank → 18 mm jaw crank) servo torque 0.080 N·m = 0.82 kg·cm MG90S @ 6 V 2.2 kg·cm → SF 2.7 SG90 @ 4.8 V 1.6 kg·cm → SF 2.0

Both pass on torque. The decision has nothing to do with torque. It is about what happens the twentieth time the gripper closes on something it cannot close on.

SG90 — nylon gears

  • 9 g, about $2
  • Strips its output gear on a hard stall or a side impact. The gripper is the part of this robot most likely to receive both.
  • Fine where load is predictable and light — which is exactly the wrist-levelling axis.

MG90S — metal gears chosen

  • 13.4 g, about $6
  • Identical 22.8 × 12.2 × 22.5 mm body and 32 mm hole spacing — drop-in either way, so the mount is one part.
  • Survives the stalls and knocks that kill an SG90. The extra $4 buys a part that does not become a consumable.

Mechanism

Two mirror-image printed jaws on M3 pins, with meshed gear sectors at their pivots — module 1.5, roughly 10 teeth of engagement, which prints cleanly on a 0.4 mm nozzle. The servo drives one jaw through a short link; the sectors force the other to mirror it. Four printed parts, two pins, self-centring, and no way to get the jaws out of sync. Jaw faces carry a 90° V-groove so pens and screwdriver shafts centre themselves, plus a glued-on rubber pad.

Design the jaws to be compliant, not just strong. A hobby servo commanded to a "closed" angle it cannot reach will sit there drawing stall current until something gives. Thin the last 12 mm of each jaw to 1.6 mm so it flexes ~1 mm under load. One closed angle then grips a range of object sizes without the servo ever hard-stalling. This detail matters more than the gear material.

06

Base stability

Tipping about the front edge of the base plate, arm fully extended, payload at maximum reach. The shoulder axis sits 10 mm forward of the base axis, so the moving CoM is 211 mm out.

// overturning, about an edge 90 mm from the base axis M_over = 0.224 × 9.81 × (0.211 − 0.090) = 0.266 N·m // restoring, base assembly only M_rest = 0.269 × 9.81 × 0.090 = 0.237 N·m → SF 0.89 TIPS // restoring, with 400 g of ballast in the base cavity M_rest = 0.669 × 9.81 × 0.090 = 0.591 N·m → SF 2.22 OK

The arm as-drawn tips over. This is the one result in the review that changes the design rather than confirming it, and it is easy to miss on paper because the arm only weighs 500 g total. A 224 g mass on a 211 mm lever beats a 269 g base on a 90 mm lever, and no amount of careful printing fixes that.

Fix: ballast the base primary

Model a sealed 66 × 66 × 18 mm cavity into the base plate with a printed snap lid. Fill with M8 washers, sand, or a bag of spare screws. 400 g minimum, 500 g preferred. Costs nothing and lowers the CoM as well as raising the mass.

Fix: park the supply on the plate

A 6 V 3 A brick weighs 150–250 g. Recess it into the base plate behind the column and it becomes free ballast plus shorter power leads. Counts toward the 400 g.

Fix: clamp it down optional

Two 8 mm slots at the rear of the plate take a small C-clamp. Makes tipping structurally impossible and is worth having for fast moves, but it should not be the only thing keeping the arm upright.

Dynamic loading is worse than this calculation. Decelerating the arm at the end of a fast reach adds an inertial term the static analysis ignores. That is why the target is SF 2.2 and not 1.2 — and why V1 firmware slew-limits every joint to 60°/s.

Workspace

Because the wrist holds the gripper level, the TCP envelope is a circle of radius L₁+L₂ = 240 mm centred not on the shoulder but on a point offset by L₃ — at (r, z) = (75, 75). Sweeping that profile through 180° of base rotation gives the usable volume.

08

Kinematics

No DH tables, no Jacobians, no numerical solvers. The base rotation decouples completely, leaving a two-link planar problem with a closed-form solution — roughly forty lines of Python including the workspace checks.

Forward

r = L₁·cosθ₁ + L₂·cos(θ₁+θ₂) + L₃ z = h₀ + L₁·sinθ₁ + L₂·sin(θ₁+θ₂) x = r·cosθ₀ y = r·sinθ₀

L₃ adds directly to r with no trig term — that is the payoff from the levelled wrist. Without it, L₃ would carry a cos(θ₁+θ₂+θ₃) factor and the inverse problem stops being closed-form.

Inverse

θ₀ = atan2(y, x) r′ = √(x²+y²) − L₃ // wrist-pivot radius z′ = z − h₀ D = (r′² + z′² − L₁² − L₂²) / (2·L₁·L₂) // |D| > 1 → target is unreachable. Check before acos(). θ₂ = −acos(D) // negative root = elbow-up θ₁ = atan2(z′, r′) − atan2(L₂·sinθ₂, L₁ + L₂·cosθ₂) θ₃ = −(θ₁ + θ₂) // slaved wrist, jaws level

The two acos roots are elbow-up and elbow-down. Elbow-down puts the joint below the table on nearly every desk-height target, so the solver takes the negative root unconditionally and never has to choose.

Validation, in order, before anything moves

  1. Reachability — |D| ≤ 1 and 130 ≤ √(x²+y²) ≤ 290.
  2. Joint limits — every θ inside its mechanical range from §01.
  3. Table clearance — z ≥ 10 mm, and the elbow itself above the table, which the elbow-up choice does not guarantee near the inner boundary.
  4. Path check — for a Cartesian move, interpolate 20 waypoints and validate each. A straight line between two legal poses can pass through an illegal one.

The accuracy limit is backlash, not mathematics. An MG996R has 1–2° of gear lash plus potentiometer deadband. At a 240 mm lever, 1.5° is 6 mm at the gripper. Expect ±5–8 mm repeatability and design around it: approach every target from the same direction so the lash is always taken up the same way, and let the 55 mm jaw opening absorb the rest. No amount of IK precision buys back a millimetre of this.

Electronics

PC ──USB──▶ ESP32 ──────5 × PWM signal──▶ servos │ ▲ └──── GND ═══════ common ══════╪═══ GND │ 6 V 3 A supply ──fuse──┬── 2 × 1000 µF ────────┘ └── V+ rail to all five servos

ESP32 — revised from an original Arduino Nano choice. The Nano's edge was native 5 V logic: hobby servos want a 5 V pulse, and a 3.3 V board introduces a question with no clean answer on paper — some servos latch reliably on 3.3 V, some jitter, and you find out after assembly. In practice the SG90/MG90S/MG996R family used here decodes PWM through an analog RC input stage that a 3.3 V high clears — a well-worn combination, not a gamble. Test each servo before final assembly; the documented fallback for a unit that jitters is a 74HCT125/74AHCT125 level shifter (~$1, one chip covers all five signal lines), not a controller change. In exchange: a dual-core chip with far more flash/RAM/GPIO than this project needs, a similar street price to the Nano, and Wi-Fi/BT already on board if V2 ever wants the arm untethered (unused in V1 either way — the PC is on the other end of a USB cable regardless). Requires the ESP32Servo library in place of the stock Servo.h; same attach()/writeMicroseconds() API, backed by the chip's LEDC PWM peripheral instead of a hardware timer.

Deliberately not included

PCA9685 driver board

Five servos fit on five ESP32 GPIOs with the ESP32Servo library. The board solves a problem — running out of timers — that starts at about twelve servos. $6 and a whole I²C bus for nothing. skipped

Encoders and current sensing

Hobby servos have no position feedback path to the MCU; adding external encoders means closing a loop the servo already closes internally. Real value, wrong project. skipped

Limit switches

Servos are absolute-position devices — they know where they are at power-on. Homing switches solve a stepper problem this arm does not have. skipped

Assembly is a small piece of perfboard on the base plate: five 3-pin servo headers, a barrel jack, the bulk capacitors, a fuse, and jumper wires to the ESP32's GPIO/GND header pins. Under an hour of soldering. Full pin-out: docs/wiring_diagram.svg.

The 7.05 A all-stall figure is not a design case — it requires every joint to be jammed simultaneously, which on a desk arm means something has already gone badly wrong. The design case is 1.8 A continuous with ~3.5 A transients at direction reversals.

Specification: 6.0 V, 3 A regulated (18 W), barrel jack, 3 A fuse 6.0 V because MG996R torque is quoted at 6 V; at 5 V you lose 15% and shoulder SF falls from 2.44 to 2.08. The 4.8–6.6 V window covers all five servos. Bulk capacitance: 2 × 1000 µF across the rail, physically at the servo headers.

Be honest about what the capacitors do. C·ΔV/I = 2000µF × 0.5V / 2A = 0.5 ms. Two thousand microfarads hold a 2 A surge for half a millisecond before the rail sags half a volt. They kill the switching spike that resets the MCU; they are not a substitute for a supply that can actually source the current. Do not let a capacitor talk you into a 1 A adapter.

Bench supply use for bring-up

Set 6.0 V with a 3 A current limit. The ammeter is genuinely diagnostic: a joint drawing 1.5 A while holding still is a joint fighting its own mechanical limit, and you will see it before you smell it. Use this for the whole of Phase 7.

6 V 3 A adapter primary

About $14. Doubles as ballast on the base plate. Buy one with a real current rating, not an optimistic one.

5 V 3 A phone charger fallback

Free if you own a USB-C PD or old 5 V/3 A brick. Costs 15% of the torque. Shoulder SF 2.08 — acceptable, not comfortable.

Three rules, each of which has destroyed someone's project.
1. Never feed the servo rail from the ESP32's 5V/VIN pin or USB. That pin sits upstream of the board's own 3.3 V logic regulator and reflects whatever the USB port can source (typically ~500 mA); five servos will brown it out and reset the board mid-move.
2. Servo ground and ESP32 ground must be joined, or the PWM has no reference and the servos twitch randomly.
3. No 7.4 V LiPo direct to the rail. It will destroy the MG90S and SG90 within minutes.

Software architecture

The split is deliberate: the firmware owns safety, the PC owns intelligence. Anything that protects the hardware lives on the ESP32 and cannot be bypassed by a buggy Python script or, later, by a confused AI agent. Everything else lives on the PC where it is easy to change.

firmware/arm_firmware/ // ESP32 (Arduino C++) — the safety layer arm_firmware.ino serial parser, 50 Hz update loop limits.h per-joint µs bounds — hard clamp, no override slew.h 60°/s rate limiter, so nothing ever slams software/robot/ // Python — the thinking layer config.py link lengths, calibration, limits (one file to tune) transport.py serial port, line protocol, timeouts, retries kinematics.py fk(), ik(), reachable(), path_safe() trajectory.py eased interpolation between poses arm.py class Arm — THE public API software/cli.py interactive shell software/sequences.py demo routines // V2 adds here and nowhere else: software/vision/ camera, detector, desk-plane homography software/agent/ natural language → calls to arm.py

Serial protocol

ASCII, line-oriented, one command per line, every command answered. Debuggable from a serial monitor with no tooling, which matters enormously on the first evening the arm does not move.

The one interface that matters

arm.home() arm.move_joints(base, shoulder, elbow) arm.move_to(x, y, z) // Cartesian, validated, interpolated arm.grip(percent) arm.pickup(x, y, z) // approach above → descend → close → lift arm.place(x, y, z)

This is the V1→V2 contract. In V2 the agent does not get its own motion code, its own limits, or its own IK — it calls exactly these six methods. Vision produces an (x, y, z); the agent picks which one; arm.py is unchanged. If V2 ever needs to modify arm.py to work, the V1 API was designed wrong.

No ROS. No behaviour trees. No message bus. A 300 mm desk arm with five servos does not have a software problem that a Python class cannot solve, and every framework added here is a week not spent making the arm work.

Mechanical design and printing

Roughly fourteen unique parts, twenty printed pieces, about 300 g of filament and 18–22 hours of print time. The links are twin side plates on printed spacers — a box section that is stiff in bending, easy to print flat with no supports, and puts every bolt in double shear.

Material assignment

PartMaterialWhy
Base plate, column, turretPLAStiffness and flatness. Nothing here is impact-loaded.
Upper arm, forearm linksPLAE ≈ 3.5 GPa vs PETG's 2.0 — 75% stiffer for the same section. Deflection is what matters in a link.
Shoulder and elbow bracketsPETGHighest bending stress in the machine, and bending is where poor layer adhesion shows up. PETG's interlayer strength is worth the small stiffness loss.
Servo horn adaptersPETGSmall, bolted, and loaded in a direction that wants to peel layers apart. The one place PETG is not optional.
Gripper jawsPETGDesigned to flex, and first to be dropped. PLA would snap at the compliant section.
Spacers, clips, trayPLAUnloaded. Print in whatever is on the machine.

Print settings throughout: 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 4 perimeters, 30% gyroid, 5 top/bottom. Perimeter count dominates strength in a thin printed part — four walls and 20% infill beats two walls and 60% every time, and weighs less.

Layer orientation — the three rules that decide whether this works

  1. Print brackets flat, never standing up. A shoulder bracket printed upright has its layer planes perpendicular to the bending stress, so the load tries to peel the layers apart and the part fails at roughly 40–60% of its in-plane strength. Printed flat, the same stress runs along continuous extrusions.
  2. Every bolt hole has its axis normal to the build plate. Bolts then clamp across layers in compression, which printed plastic handles well, rather than trying to split them.
  3. Never print a servo spline. A printed 25-tooth spline will round off — not might, will. Bolt the metal horn that came with the servo to the printed part with two or three M2 screws and let the horn take the torque. most common failure mode in printed arms

Where SolidWorks Simulation actually earns its time

Worth running 2 studies

  • Shoulder bracket — 0.44 N·m through a bolted interface with a stress concentration at the servo cutout. The one part where intuition is unreliable.
  • Upper arm link — check the elbow-servo pocket. Expect the answer to be "add a 3 mm fillet."

Not worth running

  • Everything else. The hand calculation below already answers the question.
  • Use orthotropic properties if you run it at all, and treat the result as a comparison between two designs rather than a number.
// upper-arm tip deflection, 1.3 N at 120 mm, twin 20 × 3 mm plates I = 2 × (3 × 20³)/12 = 4000 mm⁴ δ = FL³/(3EI) = 1.3 × 120³ / (3 × 2500 × 4000) = 0.075 mm

Seventy-five microns. The structure is eighty times stiffer than the servo backlash it is bolted to. Do not thicken these links. Every gram added to the arm buys nothing measurable and costs shoulder safety factor directly — this is the part of the project where over-engineering makes the robot objectively worse.

Parametric strategy for SolidWorks

  • One global variable file driving L1, L2, L3, h0, plate thickness and pin diameter across every part.
  • A servo-pocket library feature with a design table holding two rows — standard (40.7×19.7×42.9, 49.5 mm holes) and micro (22.8×12.2×22.5, 32 mm holes). Every mount references it, so swapping an MG996R for an MG90S is a configuration change, not a redesign. This is what makes Configurations A through D one robot instead of four.
  • Assembly mated to reference planes and axes, never face-to-face between links. Change L1 and the assembly rebuilds instead of erroring.
  • Configurations: Config-A-Short (L2 = 80) and Config-B-Standard (L2 = 120) from the same files.
  • 0.2 mm clearance on every pin and pocket. Print a tolerance coupon first and adjust once — it saves a reprint of every part.

How the decision was actually made

Can the cheapest configuration meet the torque requirement?
Yes — but only at 275 mm reach and 30 g payload. An MG90S elbow needs the forearm cut from 120 mm to 80 mm to reach SF 2.08. That satisfies torque by shrinking the robot out of the useful end of the brief's 250–350 mm range. conditional pass
Can university components close the gap at zero cost?
Partly. Controller, power, fasteners, wire, bearings and filament are all plausible lab stock — about $45 of the BOM. MG996R-class servos are not usually sitting in a drawer. Worth asking before ordering; not worth waiting on. partial
Is the step from A to B worth its cost?
Yes, decisively. $8 buys +40 mm reach, +25 g payload and a levelled wrist. There is no cheaper way to buy that much capability on this project. Configuration B selected. selected
Does B leave enough margin, or is D required?
Shoulder SF 2.44, elbow 5.50, base 7.3, gripper 2.7 — every joint clears the SF 2.0 floor. A DS3218 would add $18 to fix a problem that does not exist. Configuration D is not required. Revisit only if measured performance after assembly disagrees with this table. not needed

Geometry fallback — apply strictly in this order

  1. Cut the payload. 55 g → 30 g removes 39% of the shoulder moment for free. A 30 g budget still picks up every pen, USB stick and small tool on the desk.
  2. Shorten the forearm. L₂ 120 → 80 mm. One configuration change in SolidWorks, two reprinted parts, reach falls to 275 mm.
  3. Lighten the prints. 3 mm plates → 2.5 mm and 30% → 20% infill. Worth about 12 g of moving mass; deflection stays under 0.15 mm.
  4. Only then, a bigger servo. And check steps 1–3 again first.

A concrete example of why order matters. A 350 mm arm at 55 g needs about 5.4 kg·cm at the shoulder — an MG996R at SF 2.0, right at the edge, or a DS3218 for $18 more. The 315 mm arm needs 4.51 and clears it comfortably on the $8 servo. Thirty-five millimetres of reach is not worth doubling the actuator budget.

V1 → V2 roadmap

Nothing here gets built now. It is listed so that V1 does not accidentally close a door.

StageAddsCostTouches V1?
2.1 EyesUSB webcam on a printed overhead mast, bolted to the base plate$0–15One new printed part. No electronics change.
2.2 Desk-plane calibrationFour-point homography: camera pixels → desk coordinates$0New module. This is the whole of the coordinate problem — a desk is flat, so a 3×3 matrix is all you need. No depth camera, no stereo.
2.3 DetectionYOLO or a colour/contour detector returning object centroids$0New module. Feeds (u,v) into 2.2.
2.4 AgentLanguage model with arm.pickup() / arm.place() exposed as tools$0Nothing. The agent calls the same six methods the CLI calls.

The three decisions V1 makes on V2's behalf — each costs nothing now and would be expensive to retrofit: the PC holds the intelligence and the MCU holds the safety, so the agent physically cannot exceed a joint limit; the arm's public API is Cartesian, so vision output plugs straight in; and the base plate has mounting bosses for a camera mast, so the camera-to-robot transform can be calibrated once and stay valid.

"Pick up my screwdriver and put it beside my laptop" resolves to: detect → centroid → homography → arm.pickup(x, y, z) → arm.place(x′, y′, z′). Every step after the homography is V1 code that already works.

Decision gate

Four answers and the design is locked

Nothing gets implemented until you confirm these. Everything downstream — CAD, firmware, kinematics — follows from them.

  1. Configuration B? Or do you want to start at A and upgrade, which costs one reprint of the forearm.
  2. The SG90 slaved wrist — in or out? In gives level jaws for $3 and 0.13 of shoulder safety factor. Out is one fewer part and a fixed 45° bracket.
  3. What is actually in the lab? Specifically: a spare ESP32, Uno or Nano, a bench supply, M3 stock, 608 bearings, and any servos above 8 kg·cm. That answer moves the cost between $33 and $93.
  4. Is 315 mm and 55 g the right target? If you already know you want to lift a 150 g stapler, that changes the shoulder servo and it is much cheaper to change now.
